# Deductive coding of the open-ended responses

## Open-ended questions (post-study questionnaire)

1. What did you like?
2. What did you NOT like?
3. Rank the conditions from most to least preferred (virtual replicas; virtual replicas with guidance cues; virtual replicas with guidance cues and eye gaze).
4. Reason for your ranking.
5. Any other comments.

## General rules

- **Coding unit:** one participant's combined response across the "like", "dislike", "reason for ranking", and "other comments" fields.
- **Values:** 1 = the text clearly contains the claim; 0 = it does not.
- Code from the participant's words, not from the ranking positions or from inferred meaning.
- Categories are non-exclusive: one response can receive several.

## Categories

### 1. Reduced reliance on speech

The cues reduced how much the pair had to talk. This may be stated directly, or shown by a collaborator acting or checking without the partner's spoken input:

- talking became less necessary, or was used only to correct mistakes;
- the worker could place a piece without the partner's feedback or instruction;
- a collaborator could see or verify what the other was doing without asking.

Do not code a general statement that the task or partner was easy to follow with no link to replacing spoken coordination, or a wish for more verbal communication.

### 2. Head-and-gaze visualization aided awareness or presence

Both criteria are required:

1. **Reference to the head-and-gaze layer:** the response names it (eye gaze, gaze ray or cue, head avatar or visualization, or the pointer used to indicate a piece), or clearly singles out the third condition.
2. **A worker-relevant gain attributed to it:** locating a piece, seeing or anticipating the partner's next move, awareness or presence of the partner, or the third condition being easier or clearer than the second.

Negative or dismissive statements about gaze are not coded. This category is defined around worker-relevant gains, and the codebook has no matching expert-side category, so its count does not compare the two roles.

### 3. Clutter or excessive cues

The response calls a cue layer too much, too many, busy, messy, cluttered, or distracting from the task, or says it caused a loss of focus. "Confusing" or "not clear" alone is not coded.

### 4. Misregistration or real-versus-virtual confusion

The response says that the pointer or arrow did not point at the right piece, that a cue was inaccurate, misleading, or off, or that virtual and physical objects were hard to tell apart or overlapped confusingly. General latency and controller ergonomics are not coded.

### 5. Avatar or ray occlusion

The response says that the head avatar or gaze ray blocked or stood in front of the view, the pieces, or the hands.

### 6. Baseline sometimes sufficient

The response explicitly endorses the virtual-replica baseline: it was enough, sufficient, clear, or more natural; the participant spent less time or felt more relaxed with it; or ranked it best and gave a reason that favors the baseline. Calling an added layer unnecessary, with no positive statement about the baseline, is not coded.
